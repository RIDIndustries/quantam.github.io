THESIS

A quantum computer is not a faster version of a classical computer. It is a different computational model built from quantum states, quantum gates, interference, measurement, and specialized hardware.

01
THE IDEA

BIT / QUBIT

Two symbols.
Very different machinery.

Classical bits are used by familiar digital logic. Qubits are quantum systems whose states can be prepared in superposition. A quantum algorithm then applies controlled transformations before measurement produces classical data.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
NORMALIZATION: |α|² + |β|² = 1

A / 01

SUPERPOSITION

A qubit can occupy a combination of basis states. This is a property of the quantum state—not a claim that two classical answers are simultaneously readable.

A / 02

ENTANGLEMENT

Multiple quantum systems can form joint states with correlations that cannot be represented as independent classical bits.

A / 03

INTERFERENCE

Quantum amplitudes carry phase. They can add or cancel, allowing an algorithm to increase the amplitude of useful outcomes and suppress others.

A / 04

MEASUREMENT

Measurement yields a classical outcome. The point of an algorithm is to shape the state so useful outcomes have high probability when measured.

CASE STUDY

GROVER / SEARCH

Hide one answer
among eight.

Three qubits have 2³ = 8 computational basis states. We hide one. Classical search checks candidates sequentially. Grover's algorithm instead prepares a superposition, phase-marks the target with an oracle, and repeatedly amplifies its amplitude.

Why this is a useful example.

Grover's algorithm is a standard example of quantum algorithmic advantage for unstructured search: its query complexity is quadratic in the size of the search space. It does not provide “all answers at once” for free—the amplitudes must be engineered before measurement.

05 / FINAL FIELD NOTE

Not “every answer.”
The right interference.

Quantum computing is a way of using quantum mechanics as a computational resource. Superposition creates a rich state space; gates shape it; phase enables interference; measurement extracts a classical result.
